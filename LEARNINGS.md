# Learnings & Observations — US Food Recall Explorer

Consolidated from the planning/design phase and continued through the build (see `BUILD_LOG.md` for the chronological process record, and `PROMPT_LOG.md` for the underlying prompts these observations are drawn from). This file is the single source for the "learnings/observations" section of the final submission — nothing here should need repeating elsewhere.

---

## Process-level catches (see `BUILD_LOG.md` for full detail)

- Formalizing a chat discussion into a PRD via ChatPRD introduced silent scope drift — a toggle became "deferred," a one-time data pull became ambiguous "per session" language. Caught only by re-reading the generated doc line-by-line against the original conversation, not by trusting the tool's output at face value. (Full detail: `BUILD_LOG.md` Entry 0.)
- A generated low-fidelity sketch defaulted every chart type to bars, including a seasonality view that should have been a heatmap. Not caught until visually reviewed side-by-side with a cleaner reference. (Full detail: `BUILD_LOG.md` Entry 0.5.)

## What the prompt sequence itself reveals

- That same drift-catching pattern also showed up live, inside ChatPRD itself, not just afterward in review: the tool stated "excluded due to data limitations" as if it were a confirmed finding, when it was actually an untested hunch. Caught and corrected in the moment rather than carried forward as a false premise — a reminder that AI tools will confidently phrase a guess as a conclusion if the prompt doesn't explicitly separate the two.
- Iteration with ChatPRD also ran in the ordinary direction, not just correction: nice-to-haves were deliberately skipped for v1 initially, then intentionally expanded back in once the core spec felt solid — normal scope breathing, not a mistake.
- Exploring design direction in two tools in parallel (Claude Design's structural wireframes, ChatGPT's higher-level insight-led concept) surfaced complementary ideas neither tool produced alone — but reconciling them required explicit, deliberate tradeoff decisions (kept side-by-side lenses from one, adopted insight cards and chart-type choices from the other) rather than picking one tool's output wholesale.
- The event/product toggle-vs-side-by-side question surfaced independently in two places and resolved the same way both times: Claude Design's own brief initially called for a "prominent toggle," then reversed that after live review; separately, this chat's design reconciliation asked the same either/or question directly and got the same answer (side-by-side, not a toggle) — convergent evidence from two different tools/processes, not just one opinion repeated.
- ChatGPT's "can these insights be pulled in on the fly?" question is the direct origin of a hard constraint that survived into the final PRD word-for-word in spirit: computed, rule-based, never LLM-generated, never causal. A good constraint can come from a side question in a different tool entirely, not just from the primary spec document — part of why running two design explorations in parallel, rather than picking one tool and staying there, was worth the extra coordination overhead.
- Both parallel design explorations (Claude Design, ChatGPT) followed the same shape independently: open brainstorming first, the PRD introduced mid-session rather than upfront, concepts only becoming answerable against real constraints once it arrived. Neither tool was instructed to follow that pattern — it emerged the same way twice, unprompted.
- The recurring pattern across this whole planning phase — an AI-generated artifact quietly overstating its own authority (ChatPRD phrasing a hunch as a conclusion, a sketch defaulting to the wrong chart type without flagging it as a default) — showed up one final time in the kickoff prompt itself: a low-fidelity wireframe got initially described as a "source of truth," and a pair of living documents got initially described as closed. The clearest single takeaway from the whole planning phase: the artifact meant to prevent drift during the build needed the same scrutiny as everything that came before it, not an exemption from it.

## Build phase — Phase 0 (see `BUILD_LOG.md` Entry 1 for full detail)

- **The most expensive error in the project came from the one source nobody was auditing: a human assumption.** The whole planning phase was organized around catching AI drift — ChatPRD phrasing a hunch as a conclusion, a sketch defaulting to bars, the kickoff prompt overstating a wireframe's authority. Every one of those was caught by review. Meanwhile "2004–present" passed through the PRD's TL;DR, a two-page wireframe with a `2004 —— 2026` axis, and the kickoff prompt itself, unchallenged, into a build brief. The dataset starts in 2012. No tool introduced this error; every tool faithfully propagated it, because none of them had called the API and none had been asked to doubt the premise. **Review catches what a document says. It does not catch what a document assumes.** Only touching the real data caught this.

- **The two `⚠️ UNVERIFIED` flags did exactly their job, and the difference from the 2004 error is instructive.** Both were refuted — reason and category are free text, and category is worse than "messy" (13.5% unmatched, 41.5% matching multiple categories). But because they had been explicitly marked as unverified during planning, no downstream work had been built on top of them, and refuting them cost nothing. The 2004 assumption was *not* flagged, so it propagated into a design artifact. The lesson isn't "verify assumptions" — it's that **the act of writing down which claims are unverified is itself the protection**, and it only protects the claims you thought to mark.

- **The dry-run → `--limit 1` → full-run ladder earned its keep on the first attempt.** `--limit 1` fetched 1,000 rows: the page-size cap was checked in the loop but never applied to the actual request. Functionally harmless, and it would have shipped invisibly — a smoke test that silently doesn't smoke-test is worse than no smoke test, because it manufactures false confidence. The ladder was specified in the kickoff prompt as a working-style preference, not a technical requirement, and it caught a real bug anyway.

- **A sample-based check was less trustworthy than the design assumption it "corrected."** I measured ~1.4 products per event from a 1,000-row API slice and flagged the wireframe's 3.8x ratio as unrealistic. The true full-dataset figure is 3.74 — the wireframe was right. The slice was contiguous, so multi-product events were split across page boundaries and systematically undercounted. This is the inverse of every other entry in this file: not an AI artifact overstating its confidence, but a *measurement* doing so. Empirical does not mean correct; a number carries the sampling method's bias silently, and unlike prose it doesn't sound like a guess. Worth remembering that "I checked the data" is itself a claim that can be wrong.

- **Constraints in the source data quietly redesigned the app.** openFDA caps `skip` at 25,000 against a 29,278-record corpus, which forces year-windowed pagination — a naive offset loop drops thousands of rows and only errors *after* the loss. And the absence of a country-of-origin field doesn't defer the growing-season-vs-imports question, it kills it. Neither of these is discoverable from documentation or from a PRD; both changed the build. **Phase 0 wasn't a verification step that happened to precede the real work — it was the step where the real design got decided.**

## Build phase — Phase 1 (see `BUILD_LOG.md` Entry 2 for full detail)

- **A test written before the code caught a bug that a test written after would have blessed.** openFDA's `"02121207"` parses cleanly as 7 December, year 212, so `errors="coerce"` never fires. Under pandas 2.x the old `datetime64[ns]` floor coerced it to `NaT` for free; pandas 3.0's `datetime64[us]` accepts it. Written after the implementation, the test would have asserted whatever the code already did and passed. Written first, it asserted what *should* happen and failed. This is the concrete answer to "tests after achieve the same thing" — they don't, and here is the row that proves it.

- **A dependency upgrade silently removed a safety net nobody knew they were relying on.** The pandas 2.x behaviour that made absurd dates safe was never a decision — it was a side effect of a storage limit. pandas 3.0 widened the range and the protection vanished with no error, no warning, and no changelog entry anyone would connect to this app. The lesson isn't "read changelogs"; it's that **correctness resting on an unstated property of a dependency is invisible until it moves**. The plausibility window is now explicit precisely so it can't evaporate again.

- **I reproduced the exact error I had just designed the system to prevent, in the same commit.** The category ladder's whole rationale is that ingredient words must not outrank product-type words — I wrote that reasoning into a module docstring, then ranked Beef/Pork/Poultry in the top tier anyway. The premise felt different ("beef names a product") but was false for the same reason, and for a domain fact I didn't have: **FDA does not regulate meat, poultry, or processed egg products — USDA FSIS does.** Articulating a principle clearly is not the same as recognising the next instance of it. The recurring shape of this whole project is not "AI gets things wrong" but "a stated rule doesn't automatically apply itself to the next case."

- **Two silent regex bugs surfaced from one failing assertion.** `\bpie\b` doesn't match "pies"; bare `apple` matches inside "pineapple". Neither would ever throw. Both would have misfiled rows into the wrong category permanently, and no amount of eyeballing the category distribution would have revealed them — the totals looked plausible. Keyword rules fail quietly by nature, which is an argument for testing them by named example rather than by aggregate share.

- **The skip list in the testing brief was worth as much as the requirements.** Ruling out adversarial-input handling, retry tests, and rendered-output assertions prevented an entire tier of work that would have looked diligent and caught nothing, because the input is a fixed file and the renderer is a trusted library. Deciding what *not* to test is a design act, and it is the part most easily skipped in favour of coverage as a number.

- **Deciding before testing, because under TDD the test is the spec.** Both open design questions — category precedence, multi-label vs. single — were settled with a human before any assertion was written. Had I guessed, the guess would have been frozen into passing tests and become invisible: green tests read as validation, not as an unexamined assumption. This is a genuine hazard of TDD that its advocates rarely mention.

- **Honest note on discipline.** One GREEN step over-implemented: the failing test required a single category rule and I wrote thirteen. The keyword table is closer to data than to logic, but the accurate description is a lapse, and the tests written afterwards lock behaviour rather than drive it. They still earn their place — I verified the ordering guard by promoting Dairy and watching the assertion break — but recording the lapse matters more than the rationalisation for it.

## Build phase — pre-Phase-2 review

- **I characterised a bucket instead of opening it, and was wrong.** I described the 18.4% `Uncategorized` share as irreducible ambiguity and predicted diminishing returns from better rules. Twenty-five random samples refuted that in seconds: `Kippered Herring`, `Enoki Mushroom`, `Sunflower Kernels`, `Soybean Oil`, `grits`, `Tofu`. Three whole categories were missing. Expanding the rules cut it to 11.9%. The failure wasn't the estimate — it was that I produced an estimate at all when the data was one command away. **This is the third time in this project that reasoning about the data lost to looking at it**, after the 2004 date range and the meat categories. The pattern is now unmistakable enough to be a rule rather than an observation.

- **A finding can be overstated the same way a plan can.** I recorded the wireframe's top-foods chart as "not achievable" and it took a direct challenge to walk it back. The chart works exactly as drawn; only its placeholder labels differ from real data, which is what a low-fidelity mock is *for*. I had a genuine finding (the USDA jurisdiction boundary) and inflated it into a design conclusion it didn't support. Every earlier entry in this file is about an artifact overstating its authority — a PRD, a sketch, a kickoff prompt. This one is about a *conclusion* doing it, mine, and self-review didn't catch it.

- **Flagging is not asking.** The partial-2026 problem sat under "Open questions" in two build-log entries and came up three times in conversation without a decision ever being put forward. From the outside that reads as active work; in reality nothing was moving. An open-questions list is a place items go to look attended-to. Anything that actually needs a decision has to be asked as a question, with options, or it will quietly persist until it becomes expensive.

- **A test failed for a good reason, which is a category of event worth noticing.** Expanding the category rules dropped `Uncategorized` from 18.4% to 11.9% and broke the bound test asserting the documented range. Nothing was wrong — the test existed precisely to force the documentation to move with the code rather than going stale behind it. Most failing tests indicate a bug; this one indicated that a deliberately-recorded fact had changed and needed re-recording. Worth writing tests that can fail this way.

- **Derived columns launder judgment into measurement.** A bar reading "Produce 17.0%" has the visual authority of a count, but the assignment behind it is a keyword rule with known misfires. This is the real argument for keeping `Uncategorized` visible rather than hiding it: it is the only element of the chart that admits the rules are rules. It also set the boundary on what *not* to classify — distribution scope, repeat-offender status, and recall duration were all considered and rejected, because each would add a dimension of laundered judgment without serving any of the three core questions.

## Build phase — the category accuracy reckoning

- **I fabricated a documented example and called it an accepted tradeoff.** "Ice cream sandwich resolves to Bakery" was written into a module docstring, the PRD, the handoff note, and two build-log entries. I never ran it. It resolved to `Prepared/Frozen`. The mechanism is worth naming precisely: I had a *real* limitation to illustrate, needed an example, generated a plausible one, and the plausibility substituted for verification. Labelling it an "accepted tradeoff" made it worse — that framing signals *a decision was made here*, which discourages exactly the scrutiny that would have caught it. **An invented illustration of a true claim is still an invented claim**, and dressing it as deliberate makes it durable.

- **Coverage is not accuracy, and I reported one as if it were the other.** "11.9% Uncategorized" got quoted repeatedly as the quality number for the category rules. It measures how many rows got *a* label. It says nothing about how many got the *right* one. The clamshell bug — 509 rows, a fifth of the entire Seafood category, all cheesecakes and salads in plastic packaging — lived entirely inside the "successfully categorised" 88% and was invisible to the metric I was quoting. **Any metric that cannot see your worst bug is the wrong metric**, and the tell is that it was the only number available, not the number chosen.

- **Five failure classes, and no process generating them.** Ingredient-vs-product-type, meat-as-ingredient, regex substring bugs, phrase-identity shattering, packaging-as-food. Each fix was cheap and each test now guards it. The problem is the discovery method: four of five came from someone happening to look at the right rows, two of those from Mai rather than me. That is not a process, it is luck with good manners. The decision to freeze the rules rather than keep patching follows directly — **when your bug-finding method is chance, more time spent doesn't converge**, it just produces a number you still can't quote.

- **A challenge on one word was worth more than every self-review in this project.** "I don't think an ice cream sandwich is a bakery item" surfaced a fabricated claim, a systematic classification failure affecting 1,393 rows, a second failure class affecting 509 more, and the coverage-vs-accuracy confusion underneath all of it. None of that came from reviewing my own work, and I had reviewed this module several times. The reviews checked whether the code did what the comments said; they never checked whether the comments were true.

- **Deferring is cheap when the boundary is drawn right.** Freezing the regex and deferring to an LLM pass costs almost no rework, because `pipeline.py` sets `df["category"]` in one line and nothing downstream cares where it came from. That was not foresight about LLMs specifically — it was ordinary separation of derivation from consumption. **Good boundaries turn "we might replace this later" from a risk into a footnote**, and their value shows up in decisions you don't have to agonise over.

## Build phase — Phase 2, Slice 1: where session cost actually goes (see `BUILD_LOG.md` Entry 3)

- **Context remaining and session budget are two different meters, and I was only watching one.** Checking context usage mid–Phase 1 and seeing ~70% free felt like a safe signal. It wasn't predictive of the thing that actually ran out: session usage, which the platform's own breakdown attributes mostly to *how* the context was used, not how much of it was full. 54% of usage came from turns run above 150k tokens of context — caching softens the cost of a long session, it doesn't erase it, and the softening is exactly what makes the context-remaining number look reassuring while the session budget keeps draining underneath it.
- **A "heavy" skill's cost is paid again close to every time it's invoked, not once.** `superpowers:test-driven-development` alone accounted for 37% of this session's usage — more than a third, from one skill's instructions being loaded into context. That is a legitimate cost of following the skill properly (it *should* load its full checklist rather than a summary), but it means the decision to invoke a heavy skill is also a budget decision, not just a workflow one.
- **A plugin is a bundle, and the bundle's total is what counts against the limit, not any one skill in it.** The `superpowers` plugin was 51% of session usage across `test-driven-development` (37%) and `brainstorming` (14%) combined — more than half the session from one plugin's contributions. Auditing "what does this plugin actually invoke, and how often" is a real lever, separate from auditing any individual skill.
- **The fix has to be a habit at a specific handoff point, because nothing else creates that point automatically.** Neither `/model` nor `/clear` persists a rule ("always downgrade after plan mode," "always clear between phases") — each is a manual action taken once, in the moment. The natural trigger is the plan-approval boundary itself: once `ExitPlanMode` returns and execution starts, the reasoning that produced the plan is no longer load-bearing — the plan file on disk *is* the spec now, and a fresh session (or a cheaper model) can execute it exactly as well as the session that wrote it. That boundary is easy to name in hindsight and easy to miss in the moment, because the conversation just continues and nothing prompts the switch.

## Build phase — handing off the two remaining Phase 2 chart decisions

- **An abstract description of a design question got the honest answer "this is too abstract for me" — a concrete rendering of both options, from the real numbers, got a confident answer in one turn.** Asking "should the trend line split by severity or stay one total line" left Mai unsure which option she was even picking. Rebuilding the same question as two small ASCII sketches built from the actual 2016-spike numbers (Class I nearly doubling while the total looks like one bump) resolved it immediately, and surfaced a reason to prefer the 3-line version that the abstract framing hadn't carried at all. Same pattern the whole project has already established for AI-authored artifacts — a sketch, a wireframe, a real screenshot beats a description of one every time — applying here to a question I was asking, not just one I was answering. Worth defaulting to a concrete preview any time a design question has more than one plausible reading, rather than reaching for prose first and only sketching it out if prose fails.
- **A factual correction propagated through three documents unnoticed until the next slice's handoff needed the exact date.** "Partial year — through Aug 2026" and "snapshot ends 2026-08-05" were written into the PRD (twice) and `HANDOFF_PHASE2.md` during planning, before any real data existed to check them against. `openfda_last_updated` (2026-08-05) is when openFDA's source was fetched/published; the last actual `recall_initiation_date` in the snapshot is 2026-07-08, about four weeks earlier — the two dates measure different things and only one of them belongs on a chart label claiming to say when the data ends. This is the same shape as the 2004-vs-2012 date error from Phase 0: a plausible-sounding date written during planning, never checked against the data it described, propagating through multiple documents until a build step that actually touched the real dates caught it.

## What I'd want to know earlier (author's own reflections)

- **That USDA food recall data is a separate system entirely.** FDA and USDA FSIS split jurisdiction over the food supply — FDA covers most packaged and processed food, USDA covers meat, poultry, and processed egg products — and each publishes its own recall data. I picked the openFDA dataset without knowing that "US food recalls" is structurally two datasets, so the app can only ever answer the question for one half of the food supply. Not a blocker and not something to fix now: the honest move is to state the boundary in "About the data" and note **combining FDA + USDA FSIS as a future iteration**. Worth flagging as a general lesson about public data — jurisdictional splits inside a domain are invisible from the API docs and only show up when you look at what's *missing* from the results.

- **How to use an LLM to classify free text like this.** The whole food-category problem — free-text product descriptions, keyword rules that leave 18.4% uncategorized and misfire on ingredient mentions — is precisely the kind of task an LLM handles well and regex handles badly. I defaulted to keyword matching because it's what I knew, without seriously evaluating a one-time LLM labelling pass as an alternative. Worth distinguishing two very different uses of an LLM here, because the PRD's "never LLM-generated" rule applies to only one of them: generating *claims* about the data (banned, and rightly) is not the same as *labelling input rows* in a one-time offline preprocessing step whose output is frozen into the CSV, inspectable, and committed. The app would stay fully deterministic and offline either way. Something to learn properly — and directly relevant given this is a course on agentic AI.

## Tool-authored reflections (kept verbatim — strong analysis worth preserving as originally written)

**Claude Design, on its own wireframe sequence:**
"The sequence converges, diverges, then converges again. The opening brief was unusually specific about structure, so the first round produced arrangements of a single idea rather than genuinely different products... The pivot point is the PRD: it arrived only after three rounds of work, and once it was in play the concepts could be argued against real constraints... The final direction is a merge rather than a pick — the side-by-side lens structure from the concept round, the visual treatment from an outside mockup, and the compact filter row from the narrowing round — and it reverses the opening brief's own call for a prominent toggle, which is the clearest evidence that the requirement changed as it was seen rather than as it was specified."

**ChatGPT, on its own design exploration:**
"The work evolved from a fairly open-ended request for visual brainstorming into a more specific exploration of interaction models, with the PRD serving as a starting point rather than a fixed constraint. The biggest course correction was questioning the conventional filter-and-dashboard structure and considering whether the interface should instead guide users through questions or surface findings proactively. The final prompt then pushed that idea one step further, from a static insight-led presentation toward dynamic, data-derived observations that respond to the user's exploration."
